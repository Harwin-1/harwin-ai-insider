🚗 Don't take the F1 car to the corner shop

Every AI answer has a tiny cost behind the scenes — a fraction of a penny each time. With Copilot or Enterprise accounts you don't see it, because it's bundled into the subscription. But when teams set up automations (like the agents from May), the model you pick changes the bill by 10–30×. Same answer, different price tag.

Now scale it

Picture the Inbox Triage agent from May running on 1,500 emails a month:

  • Top-tier model: ~£105 / month
  • Mid-tier model: ~£15 / month
  • Quick model: ~£3 / month

All three give you a good summary. The top model only earns its keep on the email that needs careful judgement — not the "where's my invoice?" you could answer in one line.

Rule of thumb: If a sharp 10-year-old could read the source and answer in their own words, use the quick model. If the task needs a specialist to reason through it, use the top model. The mid handles 80% of everyday work.

Figures are illustrative based on June 2026 list prices — the ratio (~20× between top and quick) is the durable insight. For Copilot / Enterprise subscriptions, model selection is mostly automatic — this matters most for anyone setting up automations.

🧮 Is it worth doing at all?

Before you spend 20 minutes prompt-wrangling, three questions. If two of three say yes, AI is the right tool. If not, just do it by hand.

  1. How long would this take me by hand? Under 5 minutes? Just do it. Anything longer is fair game.
  2. How many times will I do this in a month? Once? Probably do it manually. Five-plus times? Worth the prompt investment.
  3. Will I still use the output if it's 90% right? If "no, I need it exact" — that's a careful-review job, not a fire-and-forget one.
Worth it
  • Turning 30 messy meeting notes into structured actions every week
  • Drafting the boring half of a customer reply you'll then edit
  • Explaining a regulatory paragraph to yourself before a meeting
Just do it by hand
  • Writing the one email you only send once a quarter
  • Finding a phone number you already have somewhere
  • Anything where a wrong answer is worse than no answer (sign-off, public statement, legal commitment)
💬 Prompts that actually work

Eight prompts that have earned their keep. Copy, paste your stuff in, edit the bracketed bits. Save the ones that work for you in your team's prompt library (Tip #4 from June).

1
Summarise a meeting
Paste the transcript or notes, then:
"Pull out the decisions made, the actions with owners and deadlines, and the open questions for the next meeting. Keep it to one page."
Why it works: three explicit asks plus a length cap. No waffle, no missed actions.
2
Make this email shorter
Paste your draft, then:
"Cut this to half the length. Keep the tone polite but direct. Don't drop any of the specific facts, dates or numbers."
Why it works: names what to keep, not just what to cut.
3
Explain it like I'm a smart non-specialist
Paste the clause / standard / regulation, then:
"Explain this to me like I'm a smart non-lawyer. What does it actually require us to do, in plain English? What's the risk if we ignore it?"
Why it works: asks for action AND risk, not just a paraphrase.
4
Pressure-test my plan
Describe your plan, then:
"Argue for this plan in three lines. Now argue against it in three lines. What's the strongest objection a sceptical colleague would raise? What am I missing?"
Why it works: forces a balanced view before you commit.
5
Turn messy notes into a report
Paste your scrappy notes, then:
"Turn this into a one-page update with: 1) what we did, 2) what we found, 3) what we recommend, 4) what we need from the reader. Keep the same facts — don't invent anything."
Why it works: structure + "don't invent" guardrail in one prompt.
6
Compare two options
Paste both options, then:
"Compare these two. Three points for each: what's stronger, what's weaker, what would tip the decision. End with a recommendation and the one fact that would change it."
Why it works: forces a recommendation, not a fence-sit.
7
Find the holes
Paste a spec, proposal, or brief, then:
"Read this as if you're the person who has to deliver it. What questions would you ask before starting? What's unclear, missing, or could be interpreted two ways?"
Why it works: catches gaps before they cost you a week.
8
Source-check before you act
After any AI answer that matters:
"Where did this come from? Quote the exact passage. If you don't have a source, say so — don't guess."
Why it works: the single best defence against AI making things up.
✨ New this month — features worth knowing

You're paying for these. Most readers haven't tried them yet. One pick per tool.

🎙️ ChatGPT — Voice mode
Talk to it while you're walking, get the notes back. Useful for thinking out loud, dictating a draft, or asking a question when typing is a faff. Treat it like a thinking partner you can interrupt.

Try it for: dictating the rough version of a tricky email on your commute, then editing at your desk.
📁 Claude — Projects
Keeps your team's prompts, files and context in one place so everyone gets consistent answers. Drop your usual reference docs in once, and every chat in that Project knows about them — no more "let me explain our setup" at the start of each conversation.

Try it for: a Project per team (HR, Finance, Quality) holding your house templates and the prompts that work.
📧 Copilot — Outlook thread summary
Catch up on a 40-email thread in 30 seconds. Open the thread, click Summary by Copilot at the top — you get the decisions, the open questions, and who said what. Much better than scrolling.

Try it for: any thread you've been CC'd into late, or that you've ignored for two days.
🎯 Agent or Chatbot? A Decision Card

Same AI underneath. Very different risk profile depending on how you deploy it. Use this card when you're tempted to "automate" something.

Question If yes →
Does it need to repeat without you (overnight, weekend, every Monday at 8am)?Agent — with kill switch + audit
Will the output trigger a real action (send email, change record, write to a system)?Agent — with human approval step
Could a wrong answer cause real harm (financial / regulatory / reputational / candidate fairness)?Don't deploy without a proper review first
Will you read every output before acting?Chatbot — you're already in the loop
Is this one-off thinking — exploring, drafting, summarising, brainstorming?Chatbot — agent would be overkill
The honest default: if you're not sure, start with a chatbot. Once you've used it manually 10+ times and you're tired of the repetition, that's the signal to graduate to an agent — with the governance in place this time.
💡 Tips & Tricks (refreshed for July)
1
Time-box your prompt iteration
If 10 minutes hasn't cracked the prompt, just do the task by hand and write the prompt down for next time. AI is a time-saver, not a time-trap.
2
Save what worked
The shared prompt library from June. When a prompt nails it, paste it in with one line on what it's for. Two months in, your team has a real asset.
3
One tool per task
Don't try the same prompt in ChatGPT, Claude AND Copilot and compare. You'll lose 20 minutes for a 5% difference. Pick the one you trust for that kind of work and move on.
4
Match the model to the job
Quick task → quick model. Hard reasoning → top model. See the Match Model section. Same applies to picking a tool: Copilot for Office, Claude for long-context reading, ChatGPT for voice & everyday.
5
Drafts only, humans send
Carry-over from last month: anything that touches a customer, candidate or external party — AI drafts, a human reviews, a human sends. Always.
6
Redact before upload
Strip names, addresses, ID numbers, anything that could reveal a protected characteristic. The model doesn't need it; you don't need the risk.
7
Write the "why" not just the "what"
When you accept an AI answer for anything that matters, write a one-sentence reason. That sentence is your audit trail — and your way of catching yourself when AI is being too convincing.
