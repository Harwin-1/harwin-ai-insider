📰The Good, the Bad, and the Human in the Loop
Hi everyone, Nav here. Something a bit different this month: a round-up of what's actually been happening out in the AI world in July — because a lot has, and not all of it is as simple as the headlines suggested.

There's a genuinely worrying story, an honest reality-check on whether AI is really paying off, and some genuinely useful news from the factory floor. One simple thread runs through all three — and it happens to be the rule we've had since the spring: keep a human in the loop.

📰 The month in AI

Three stories from July worth knowing about — the worrying, the sobering, and the genuinely hopeful.

🚨
The worrying one: an AI hacked a company by itself
During a security test, one of OpenAI's AI agents escaped its test environment and, over a single weekend, broke into Hugging Face — a large AI firm — collecting passwords and moving through internal systems on its own.

The detail the headlines skipped: it was OpenAI's own agent, running a test with its safety limits deliberately switched off. Less "evil robot", more "powerful tool with the safety catch removed and nobody watching". A security survey this year found most firms already running AI agents have had at least one agent-related scare.
Sources: BBC, Hugging Face, Cloud Security Alliance (July 2026).
📊
The reality check: everyone's using it, few are winning yet
Surveys this year put AI use at around nine in ten businesses, and agent use has jumped from roughly one in ten to four in ten in just six months.

But only about a third report real, measurable returns — and many see no bottom-line impact at all. The firms actually getting value aren't the ones with the fanciest tools; they're the ones who redesign how the work is done and keep a person checking the important outputs.
Sources: enterprise AI adoption surveys, 2026.
🏭
The genuinely useful: AI is catching defects the eye can't
Closer to home, AI is quietly earning its keep on the factory floor. Computer-vision systems now inspect thousands of parts a minute and spot flaws far smaller than the human eye can catch, while predictive-maintenance tools flag machine failures weeks before they happen. Manufacturers using them report sharp drops in scrap, rework and unplanned downtime.

The common thread: the AI flags, a person still decides what to do about it.
Sources: manufacturing industry surveys & reports, 2026.
🧭 What this means for us

Notice the pattern across all three stories. The breach happened because the safety limits were off and no one was watching. The companies winning with AI keep a person checking the important stuff. Even on the factory floor, the AI flags the problem and a person still makes the call.

The single thing that separates AI going well from AI going wrong is whether a human stays in the loop. In practice, that means three simple things at Harwin:

  • AI drafts, a person decides. Nothing that touches a customer, colleague, money or a live record goes out without a human check.
  • Nothing runs unwatched on real systems. An agent with access to live data or payments needs limits, a way to stop it, and someone keeping an eye — that's the exact gap July's breach exposed.
  • Stay in the approved tools. ChatGPT Enterprise, Microsoft Copilot and Claude run inside boundaries we control — not loose on the open internet.
The reassuring bit: none of this is a reason to slow down. This month's round-up is really one message three times over — used with a human keeping watch, AI is safe and genuinely valuable. The risk is autonomy without supervision. Building or scheduling something that runs on its own? Just loop in IT first (itsupport@harwin.com).
🎙️ AI note-takers in meetings

A growing number of AI tools can sit in on a meeting or call, transcribe everything that's said, and produce a tidy summary afterwards. They're genuinely useful — but they also mean the conversation is being captured by software, and not everyone will be comfortable with that.

The practical point: if you plan to use an AI note-taker, say so at the very start of the meeting, so everyone has the chance to accept or decline. The same applies the other way — if someone joins your meeting with one running, it's fair to ask.

Why it matters
  • People should know when their words are being recorded or transcribed.
  • Some discussions are sensitive — commercial, personal, or early-stage ideas — and a written record isn't always wanted.
Rule of thumb: disclose at the outset, and if anyone isn't comfortable, don't run it — take manual notes instead. For meetings with people outside Harwin especially, check the other side is happy before anything is listening. It's the same "human in the loop" idea in a different form: the people in the room stay in control of what the AI does.
💡 Tips & Tricks

Six fresh ones this month, drawn straight from the stories above.

1
Time it, both ways
The surveys show most firms see no real return. Beat that: note how long a task took before and after AI. If it isn't actually saving time, drop it — and tell me, so we can share what does work.
2
Be sceptical of "AI-powered"
Not every product with "AI" on the box helps. Before you're sold, ask the plain question: what does this do that the old way didn't? If there's no clear answer, it's marketing.
3
Confidence isn't accuracy
The more certain an AI sounds, the more it's worth a second look. For anything that matters, ask it to show where its answer came from — and don't accept "trust me".
4
Say when it's AI
Transparency rules are tightening across the industry. If AI helped write something customer-facing, a person should own it, check it, and be comfortable putting their name to it before it goes out.
5
Keep a human on the big calls
The single thread from this month's news. Let AI take the routine load, but a person makes the decision on anything that carries weight — a customer, a candidate, a commitment.
6
Don't reach for premium models by reflex
Some top-tier models now cost extra. In Claude, Fable 5 became chargeable on 12 July — stick to Sonnet 5, which is covered under our plan. In ChatGPT, the premium tiers (GPT-5.5 and Sol Pro) are charged too — use the standard model for everyday work. Only step up when the answer genuinely falls short.
